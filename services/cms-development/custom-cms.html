Custom CMS Development

A CMS Designed Around the Way Your Team Actually Publishes

Sometimes the problem is not that a CMS lacks features. It is that the platform expects your team to work in a way that does not match the business. Shivantra develops custom content-management solutions for specialized workflows, permissions, data relationships and integrations where a standard CMS configuration would create more friction than it removes.

Discuss Your CMS Workflow
When a standard CMS becomes a constraint

Custom CMS development should solve a real limitation

A mature CMS can handle many website requirements.

Custom development is usually justified only when the way your organization manages content is itself unusually specific. Examples include:

Specialized workflows

Content may move through several business-specific stages before it can be published.

Granular permissions

Access may depend on region, department, account, content type or another business rule.

Complex content relationships

Content may be connected to operational data, customers, products, locations or records that do not fit a normal page/post model.

Multi-tenant publishing

Different organizations or branches may manage their own content within one platform.

Domain-specific editor experience

Editors may need forms, terminology, validations and actions designed around the industry rather than generic CMS fields.

Deep system integration

The content-management interface may need to read or update ERP, CRM, databases or internal systems.

Build, customize or integrate?

Custom software is one option, not the default answer

Before building a custom CMS, we compare three approaches.

Configure

Configure an existing CMS

Best when the content and workflow fit standard capabilities with sensible configuration.

Extend

Extend a headless or traditional CMS

Best when the core platform works but needs custom schemas, workflows, UI extensions or integrations.

Build

Build a custom CMS

Best when editorial operations are deeply tied to proprietary business rules or the CMS is part of a broader internal platform.

The correct decision considers:

  • implementation cost
  • operating cost
  • maintenance
  • vendor dependence
  • internal capability
  • security
  • future requirements
  • editor experience
Content model

Model the information before building the interface

A custom CMS should not start with screens.

It should start with the data and relationships the team needs to manage.

The final model may include:

  • validation
  • references
  • taxonomies
  • versions
  • ownership
  • status
  • localization
  • permissions

Workflow

Make the publishing process visible in the system

If content moves through a defined process, that process can be represented in the CMS. A workflow might include:

  • draft
  • internal review
  • legal or compliance review
  • market review
  • approved
  • scheduled
  • published
  • archived

Transitions can be restricted according to roles. Where appropriate, the system may also trigger:

  • notifications
  • tasks
  • webhooks
  • downstream updates

Editor experience

Give editors an interface that speaks their language

A custom CMS can replace generic controls with a domain-specific interface. That can include:

  • business terminology
  • conditional fields
  • guided forms
  • validation
  • inline reference selection
  • calculated fields
  • bulk actions
  • custom dashboards
  • role-specific navigation
  • previews
  • content status

The goal is not to make the CMS visually elaborate.

It is to reduce mistakes and make the common task easier.

Integrations

Content management can sit inside a larger business process

Content may already exist in another system. Or information managed in the CMS may need to trigger or update something elsewhere. A custom CMS can connect with:

  • ERP
  • CRM
  • product databases
  • digital asset systems
  • authentication
  • translation services
  • internal APIs
  • external APIs
  • marketing systems
  • publishing endpoints

For example, product specifications might remain owned by the ERP while marketing descriptions are managed in the CMS.

The implementation should make ownership clear rather than duplicating all information into one database.

Security and permissions

Access should follow responsibility

Custom CMS projects should define:

  • who can log in
  • what each role can view
  • what each role can edit
  • what each role can publish
  • which data is restricted
  • how administrative actions are audited where required

Technical implementation should use least-privilege access and enforce permissions on the server side for protected operations.

Do not rely only on hiding buttons in the interface.

Architecture

Build only the custom layers you actually need

A custom CMS might include:

  • web-based admin interface
  • backend/API
  • database
  • authentication
  • file storage
  • search
  • workflow engine
  • notification service
  • frontend website or portal
  • integration layer

Not every project needs all of these.

The architecture should remain proportional to the problem.

Example use cases

Where a custom CMS can make sense

Multi-branch publishing

Each branch manages local information while corporate teams control shared content.

Product information publishing

Marketing content is combined with product data owned by another system.

Regulated content workflow

Content requires defined review or approval before publication.

Partner content platform

Different partner organizations maintain their own approved content.

Internal publishing portal

Teams manage structured content that feeds several business applications.

Industry directory

Editors manage complex records, relationships, verification status and publication rules.
